Gaming-informed therapy

The premise

Method, not destination

Gaming is an occupation

When a referral mentions gaming, the reflex is often "reduce screen time". We start somewhere else. For many clients gaming carries identity, community, mastery and connection. Treat it with that respect and it becomes something you can assess, grade and build on.

The real goals sit underneath

Gaming is the delivery method. Under the play sit the actual targets: emotional regulation, communication, self-care, transitions, education, employment and independent living.

If we treat gaming with the respect of a real occupation, the level of analysis we can do to support it, and use it as a bridge to other occupations, becomes a genuine opportunity.

Rapport toolkit

Rapport is the whole game

We gravitate to people like us, or who we want to be. Build real connection and the client becomes open to goal-setting. Miss it and every technique falls flat. Tap a technique to open it.

01Lead with your own gamer identity

Share your Steam profile, the games you have played, your achievements, what you are grinding right now. Telling a client you have platinum trophies in Elden Ring and Sekiro signals the same cultural background instantly. Most clients have never met a therapist like that.

02Sit shoulder to shoulder, not across the desk

Co-op play replaces "I am the therapist, here is what you will do next" with "I am another gamer, let us move forward together". Liam calls it power-levelling alongside the client.

03Let them be the expert

Ask the client to teach you their game: their best techniques, the hacks, the lore. Being coached by the client flips the usual power dynamic, and it is often better than picking their single favourite game.

04Use "beginner" as an advantage

Not a gamer yourself? Say so honestly: "I am a complete noob at this, but I have just started grinding it, show me how to get better." That invitation to teach builds rapport without any pretending.

05Be authentic, never fake it

Clients detect inauthenticity immediately, and cynical gamification can backfire badly. Genuine passion, even for completely different games, earns respect. The practice is named after the Estus Flask, the healing item in Dark Souls. That authenticity is the point.

06Meet them at their engagement level

Some clients start with typing only, join voice chat after four or five sessions, then video, then in person. Forcing eye contact early is exhausting and can end therapy before it starts. Side-by-side play removes that pressure.

07Reframe gaming for parents

Position gaming as connection, identity and a possible pathway, not time to be reduced. Supporting it rather than fighting it can lower household arguments and strengthen relationships.

08Use nostalgia with older clients and parents

Thirty years of gaming (Master System, Mega Drive, SNES, N64, PS1) gives you shared history to draw on. Retro co-op is a fast rapport builder across generations.

On content boundaries. When a client plays something age-inappropriate, role-model rather than police it: "I don't play that one, it's too violent for me," or "it makes me motion sick. Here are the games I do play, which do you want to try?" Being clear about your own boundaries teaches more than a rule ever will.

Session prep

Small hinges swing big doors

This is not "just playing video games with kids". The first thing on the table is everything around the play. In-game performance is a fast, visible window into the basics, and the basics carry into every part of life.

The basics firstBefore play

Self-care is the on-ramp

Every step of getting ready to play is a legit target: showering, clean clothes they actually want to wear, choice and control over that, doing their own washing, making their own meals. You are not adding self-care. You are removing friction between the client and the thing they want to do.

Treat the gamer like an athlete

Elite performance in any focused task depends on the run-up: food, water, environment, a clean transition into deep focus. Teach that set-up through gaming and it carries straight into schoolwork, study and office work.

No way you study well if you haven't slept. Sort the small hinges first, then everything else has a chance. See the Small Hinges programs.

Transitions

Getting in and out without a fight

Getting into the activity and getting out of it are two of the hardest moments in any session. For this client group, the transition is often the therapy.

During play

Talk side by side

Side-by-side play is the low-pressure version of walking and talking. Attention sits on a shared task, so natural breaks open up to raise a topic, notice a reaction, or let the client talk.

Ending

Let the loop do the work

Pick games whose rhythm fits the session. Round and run-based games give clean stopping points. Nightreign runs in about 40-minute loops: near perfect for an hour with time to debrief and write notes.

The skill

A graded on-ramp

Each rung below is a real goal. For a client who doesn't leave the house, the car ride, the lights, the noise and the movement of coming to clinic is a major milestone.

Clinical reasoning

What sits under the play

Gaming is the visible layer. Underneath is standard, defensible OT reasoning. This is what goes in your notes and reports. Tap a framework to open it.

01PEO: Person, Environment, Occupation

The backbone for analysis and intervention. Assess the person (sensory sensitivities, communication needs), the environment, and the occupations that fit, then grade and adapt across all three.

02Michon's model (from driving OT)

Three altitudes of planning that map onto therapy: strategic (long-term goals and direction), tactical (how you set up the session), and operational (moment-to-moment choices in play).

03The gaming profile and baseline

Map the client's typical play style and volume, then watch for drift. A shift from high-energy competitive play to low-pressure games, or a drop in playtime, can be an early signal of burnout, low mood or anxiety, especially for clients who find it hard to name feelings (alexithymia) or read internal states (interoception).

04Motivational interviewing

Used heavily in session to help clients identify and move toward their own goals rather than imposed ones.

05ACT, with elements of CBT

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy underpins much of the psychological work.

06Sensory and executive-function profiles

Formal profiling (sensory profiles, and executive-function profiles such as Brown's) sets the baseline and directly informs session set-up.

07Boundaries versus rules

A boundary is something you protect for yourself: how you respond to a situation. A rule is something you impose on others. Teaching the difference, and how to communicate needs clearly, is one of the biggest levers for emotional regulation. Multiplayer servers are a live laboratory for it.

08Circle of control, and family systems

What sits inside a client's control, what sits in their influence, and what belongs to the wider system. Big factors in what you target and what you leave alone.

09Meta-goals and goal-laddering

Hold the long-term goals (social engagement, education, vocation, independent living) alongside the next three sessions' work. Then use motivation like gravity: find the occupation the client actually wants, and let it pull the other tasks along beneath it. For many clients the barrier is not skill, it is priority.

Working as a team

Outcomes live in the in-between

In-game rapport is only part of the work. Outcomes come from everything around the session, so engage the whole system around the client.

The client

Are they here willingly, or has therapy been imposed? Adults often name their own goals; many paediatric clients are sent. Who is driving the outcome changes how you communicate.

Parents and family

The day-to-day environment. Run separate, offset parent and stakeholder meetings and term-based check-ins so goal-setting stays aligned.

School

Or, for school refusal, staged community engagement that works back toward returning.

The wider clinical team

Psychology, speech, physio, and especially exercise physiology. Clients who get EP alongside OT and speech fill the buckets (fitness, rest, nutrition, hydration) that everything else depends on.

Funders and NDIS

And in return-to-work, the insurer, employer and treating medical team.

Advocacy

Be the translator

Clients get a stream of negative feedback about gaming. Translate what's really happening in the game (leadership, problem-solving, communication, persistence) into language parents, schools and funders understand.

Parents

"Screen time is evil"

Meet it with curiosity, not defence. Compared to what? If a client games a lot, ask what else is going on and what changed in the last three months. High volume may be a symptom to understand, not the problem.

Between sessions

The in-between community

Our Discord and Minecraft server give clients graded moderation roles. Putting someone who has never led into leadership, setting rules and handling a rule-break, builds real skills you can reflect on in session.

Why co-op

Cooperative, not competitive

Shoulder to shoulder, low pressure

Shared focus removes the intensity of face-to-face and lets conversation happen naturally.

Built-in conversation breaks

Co-op play creates natural pauses to weave in therapeutic talk or a goal-related topic.

Endlessly gradable

Change the game, the difficulty, or the rate of progression to match skill and goals.

Communication made visible

In Overcooked, the scoreboard literally shows the difference between communicating and not.

It avoids the competitive shift

PvP instantly changes the tone. Keep competitive play for between sessions.
