The case

Why games work in therapy

Interest-based engagement

Many neurodivergent brains run on interest. A game they love brings the focus we'd otherwise have to fight for.

Low-stakes practice

Mistakes in a game cost little. That makes it a safe place to try new skills.

Rich observation

We see planning, frustration, teamwork and communication live, not just hear about them.

Connection

For isolated teens, a shared world is often the easiest way into friendship.

Between sessions

The server is a space to practise skills between sessions, not just in them.

Respect for their world

Taking their interest seriously builds trust fast. It says "your world matters".

Session shape

Run a gaming session

The game is the context. The goal is still OT.

  1. Check inOut of game

    Do

    A quick check-in before logging on: energy, mood, what happened this week.

    Say it like

    "How's your battery today?"

  2. Set the missionGoal in game terms

    Do

    Turn the OT goal into a mission. Planning goal becomes "plan the castle before we build".

    Say it like

    "Today's mission: sketch the build before placing a block."

  3. Play and noticeNarrate, observe

    Do

    Play alongside. Notice and name skills as they happen. Coach in the moment.

    Say it like

    "You just changed plans when that failed. That's flexible thinking."

  4. DebriefBridge to life

    Do

    What worked? Where else would that help? Link it to home, school or work.

    Say it like

    "Where else do you need a plan before you start?"

  5. Save pointTransition out

    Do

    Warn early, stop at a natural end, and land in a calm close.

    Say it like

    "Five minutes. Let's find a good place to save."

Regulation

Sensory setup

Games can be a lot. Tune the world to the person.

Visuals

Lower graphics, brightness and particles. Reduce flashing effects.

Sound

Turn down or off music and specific sounds. Headphones help some, not all.

Mode

Creative mode removes threats and hunger. Good for early sessions and overloaded days.

Scope

One task area at a time: building, exploring or crafting. Not all three.

Breaks

Planned breaks, and a quiet spot in the world to retreat to.

Chat

Limit chat or voice when social load is high. Text can be easier than voice.

Responsibility

Teen moderators

For clients 16 and over. Responsibility is a real skill, and moderating is real responsibility.

Who

Older teen clients with a goal around responsibility, leadership or work readiness.

What they do

Welcome new players, answer questions, help keep the rules, flag problems to a clinician.

Supervision

Clinicians stay in charge. Teen mods never handle serious issues alone.

Link to goals

Debrief their moderating in sessions. It's evidence for work readiness and NDIS reviews.

Screen time conversations

Talking to parents

Parents often arrive worried about gaming. Start with curiosity, not a lecture.

Do

  • Ask what they playWhat, who with and why matters more than how long.
  • Name the benefitsConnection, regulation, mastery and a place they feel competent.
  • Work on the switchMost fights are about stopping. Use the Transition Scaffolding plan.
  • Agree the warning signsLost sleep, distress when stopped, dropping other interests.

Don't

  • Count hours onlyTwo hours with friends is different from two hours alone at 2am.
  • Use the game as a weaponTaking it away as punishment can break trust and remove their main regulation tool.
  • Dismiss their worrySleep and school concerns are real. Plan for them.
  • Promise outcomesWe use games as a context for goals, not a cure.
