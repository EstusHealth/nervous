Why "meh" lasts longest

Region-beta paradox

Really bad problems get fixed fast, because they hit a trigger that makes us act. Mildly bad ones never hit the trigger, so they hang around for years.

Quick test: is it annoying but not bad enough to act on?

The classic example: you walk any trip under 2 km and cycle anything longer. So a 1.9 km trip takes you longer than a 3 km one. Slide to see it.

Walk (12 min per km)Cycle (4 min per km)

Mildly badLingers

Really badGets fixed

Name the meh.List the small things everyone just works around.
Lower the trigger.Decide ahead: "three annoyances and I fix it."
Would I choose this today?If not, it's only staying because it's not bad enough.

From Daniel Gilbert and colleagues (2004). Links to signal vs noise (a small annoyance that keeps repeating is signal) and environment (fix the setup once, stop paying the small cost daily).

Speed of the whole system

Bottleneck

The slowest step sets the speed of everything. Speed up any other step and nothing changes.

Quick test: where does work pile up and wait?

Try it. This clinic can only see as many new clients as its slowest step allows. Speed up a step and watch the output.

The dashed line is the real output. Only one bar touches it.

Find it.Look for the queue. Work waits in front of the bottleneck.
Feed it.Never let the slowest step sit idle or do work someone else could do.
Find the next one.Fix it and a new slowest step appears. That's normal.
When the output feeds back in

Feedback loops

Sometimes the output becomes the next input. Then the system feeds itself. Good loops snowball. Bad loops spiral.

Quick test: does the result make the same thing more likely next time?

What to measure

Lead vs lag

Lag: the result you want. You only see it after. Lead: the thing you can do now that predicts the result.

Quick test: can you change it this week? Then it's a lead.
Lag (the result)Shows up later

Lead (what you control)Do it now

Track the lead every week. Check the lag every month or quarter. Lead is upstream of lag.

Which feedback matters

Signal vs noise

Signal: a real pattern worth acting on. Noise: random ups and downs. One data point is usually noise. A trend is signal.

Quick test: has it happened more than once, from more than one source?
NoiseNotice it, don't react

SignalAct on it

Stick with it long enough to know

Rule of 100

Do the core action 100 times before you judge whether it works. Most people quit after a handful of tries, long before the results can tell them anything.

Quick test: have you done it 100 times yet?

0/ 100

Count inputs, not outputs.Reps are yours to control. Results come later.
Volume first, polish later.Early reps teach you more than planning does.
Then judge.At 100 you have data. Before that you have feelings.

From Alex Hormozi. Links to lead measures (count the action), signal vs noise (100 reps shows a trend) and the infinite game (stay in long enough to learn). Your count saves in this browser only.

Flip the question

Inversion

Instead of "how do I make this work?", ask "what would guarantee it fails?" Then stop doing those things. Failure is often easier to spot than success.

Analogous thinking

Inside vs outside view

The inside view looks only at your case: your plan, your client, your effort. It's almost always too hopeful. The outside view asks "what is this like?" Find similar cases, see how they usually went, start there, then adjust for what's truly different.

Quick test: can you name three similar cases and how they turned out?

Start outside, then nudge

Which game are you playing?

Finite vs infinite games

A finite game has a finish line and a winner. An infinite game has no end. You can't win it. The goal is to keep playing, and keep the game going for others.

Quick test: is there a finish line? If not, you're in an infinite game.

Business, health, parenting and careers are infinite games. Trouble starts when you play them with a finite mindset: chasing a win in a game that never ends.

Five habits of an infinite player

Just causeA reason to exist bigger than any single win.

Worthy rivalA competitor you learn from, not one you just try to beat.
FlexibilityWilling to change the whole plan to serve the cause.

Idea from James Carse's Finite and Infinite Games, made popular by Simon Sinek's The Infinite Game. Ties in with thinking in decades, lead measures and second-order effects.
