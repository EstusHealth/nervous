GP referral pathways for driver assessment

GPs already have the problem. Fitness to drive is a hard call for them, and the WA medical form has a box for an OT driving assessment. You aren't selling. You're the easy answer to a form they dread.

M107A

Your door in

WA's Medical Assessment Certificate asks the GP if the patient needs a specialist assessment, and lists an OT assessment (which may include a driving assessment) as an option. Every GP doing driving medicals meets this question. Be the name they write in.

Borrowed from physios and psychologists

Five rules that do most of the work

RULE 1

Make referring easy

One-page "when to refer" guide, a referral template, fees, how booking works. Physio referral playbooks call these value assets.

RULE 2

Report back, always

GPs refer more to clinicians who keep them updated. Fast acknowledgement, then a one-page summary on top of the full report.

RULE 3

Win the whole practice

Practice managers decide who gets in. Reception and nurses steer patients. Learn their names too.

RULE 4

Teach, don't pitch

Education earns access. A good lunch and learn counts toward GP CPD education hours, so it gives them something they need.

RULE 5

Small and consistent

Start with 5 to 10 practices. Quarterly check-ins. Consistency beats frequency.

YOUR EDGE

A niche they can remember

"The OT for young neurodivergent drivers and late-diagnosed adults." Specific beats general. They'll remember the one patient you fit.

30 min

Fit their day, not yours

Ask to join an existing practice meeting or lunch. Twenty minutes of talk, ten of questions. Bring lunch, leave the kit. Budget $150 to $250 for catering and don't worry about cost per lead.

Run sheet

The 30 minutes

00:00
Hello and why you're here. One line on who you are and the patients you see. Thank them for the time.
02:00
Open with a case. Read Josh's case from the GP module. Ask: "Would you sign him off?" Let them talk. This is the hook.
07:00
Three domains. Driving needs sensory, cognitive and motor skills. Screening tests alone don't predict on-road safety. That gap is what an OT assessment fills.
12:00
When to refer. Walk the red flags grid. Stress the reassuring bit: a diagnosis alone does not mean unfit.
17:00
What happens after referral. Clinic part, on-road part, what the report tells them, how fast it comes back.
20:00
How to refer. Show the one-pager, the template, fees, NDIS. "Tick the OT box and send it to me."
22:00
Questions. Ask them: "What makes driving medicals hard for you?" Write it down. That's your next lunch topic.
29:00
Leave the kit. Attendance sheet for CPD, kit on the table, thank the practice manager by name.

When to refer for an OT driving assessment

Neurodivergent learners, late-diagnosed adults and the M107A question you aren't sure how to answer.

Teaching areas
  • What an OT driving assessment is, and what it adds to your clinical assessment
  • Neurodevelopmental conditions and driving: function, not label
  • Red flags that should trigger a referral
  • How to refer in WA, and what comes back to you
Tips and traps
  • A diagnosis is not a verdict. AFTD 2022 states an autism or neurodevelopmental diagnosis does not by itself compel a person to report. Reporting depends on symptoms likely to affect safe driving.
  • Assess all three domains: sensory, cognitive and motor.
  • Screening tests alone don't predict on-road safety. A composite battery plus an on-road check gives a better picture.
  • Self-report is unreliable. Corroborate with a parent, partner or driving instructor.
  • Not just pass or fail. An OT assessment can recommend conditions, specialist lessons or vehicle changes so people keep driving safely.
  • Older drivers in WA: annual medical from 80 (M108A). You can request a practical driving assessment if unsure.
Consider referral when
You can't tick fit or unfitYou're unsure on the M107A or M108A. That uncertainty is the referral.
Someone is worriedParent, partner, instructor or the patient reports near misses, getting lost, freezing or new fines.
A learner is stuckLots of logged hours, little progress, high anxiety or overwhelm in traffic.
Change across the domainsVision, attention, processing speed, coordination or strength has shifted.
A new neurological eventStroke, brain injury or a new diagnosis with functional impact.
Conditions might helpDaylight only, local area, automatic, or vehicle modifications.
An NDIS driving goalA report helps the participant get lessons or modifications funded.
Not neededA diagnosis alone, with no signs that driving is affected.
