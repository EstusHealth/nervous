Two branches, one body

Your body runs an automatic control system you never have to think about. It has two branches that pull in opposite directions. Read the basics below, then drag the slider or pick a real-life trigger to watch it play out.

An accelerator and a brake

Think of a car. One branch is the accelerator, the other is the brake. You always have a foot on both, a little. How hard you press each one sets how revved up or wound down you feel, and your body changes to match.

Parasympathetic

The brake

"Rest and digest"

Job
Save energy, digest food, repair and recover.
You feel
Calm, heavy, sleepy, full.
Body
Slow heart, slow deep breaths, small pupils, busy gut, watery mouth.
Pressed by
Lying down after a meal, long slow exhales, feeling safe, sleep.
Main nerve
The vagus, running from the brainstem down to the belly.
Sympathetic

The accelerator

"Fight or flight"

Job
Get you ready to act: move, fight, run, focus.
You feel
Alert, buzzy, tense, on edge.
Body
Fast pounding heart, quick breaths, big pupils, gut on hold, dry mouth, sweat.
Pressed by
Exercise, a scare, coffee, deadlines, pain, noise.
Main nerves
A chain of nerves beside the spine, plus adrenaline from the adrenal glands.

Not an on/off switch. Both run all day. What you feel comes from the mix.

Neither is good or bad. You need the accelerator to get out of bed and the brake to fall asleep.

The ends are extremes. Too much brake means fainting or shutdown. Too much accelerator means panic. Health is moving through the middle and coming back.

Putting it to work · for parents

A day on the dial

Load stacks up across the day like a battery draining. The same request lands differently at 7am, 3:30pm and 8pm. Here's what a typical day can look like, what changes with supports, and what to try at each point.

1. Read the state first

Is your child revved up, flat and foggy, or in the window? The fix is different for each.

2. Regulate, relate, then reason

Calm the body first, connect second. Talking, teaching and consequences only land once they're back in the window.

3. Your body is a tool

Kids borrow calm from the adults around them (co-regulation). A slower voice and a long exhale from you comes first.

Revved up?

Bring it down

Deep pressure (hugs, heavy blanket, beanbag). Heavy work (carrying, pushing, climbing). Slow rocking or swinging. Dim light, less noise. Fewer words. Long exhales. Warm drink or bath.

Flat or foggy?

Lift it up

Movement first (jumping, trampoline, animal walks). Cold water or icy drink. Crunchy or sour snack. Fresh air and daylight. Upbeat music. A favourite interest to spark them up.

Meltdown or shutdown?

Keep it safe, wait

Space and safety first. Almost no words. Stay close and calm without demands. Don't problem-solve now. Talk about it later, if at all, once they're back in the window.

Everyone's sensory profile is different. What calms one child alerts another, so test and watch.

The heart lives on a brake

A heart with no nerve input beats about 100 times a minute. The vagus nerve holds most people at 60 to 70 at rest. Many speed-ups are just the brake coming off.

Not a switch

Both branches fire together most of the time. Health looks like flexible shifting between them, which shows up as heart rate variability.

Body leads, mind follows

Signals travel both ways. Around 80% of vagus fibres carry information from body to brain, which is why breathing and posture can change how you feel.
