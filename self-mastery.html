    Read this first

    The one big idea

    Across all six books, the best-supported idea is simple. Change the situation, not your willpower. Set up the room, the calendar and the people around you so the right thing is the easy thing.

    1Environment firstMake the good thing easy and the bad thing hard before you need willpower.
    2If-then plans"If X happens, I will do Y." One of the strongest tools in behaviour science.
    3Judge the decisionA good call can still go badly. Judge the process, not the result.
    4Kind honestyOwn the truth. Talk to yourself like a good coach, not a drill sergeant.
    Evidence grades

    How strong is the science?

    • Strong Meta-analyses or big replicated trials.
    • Moderate Several good studies, some limits.
    • Weak Little direct testing. Low risk to try.
    • Myth The evidence says otherwise.

    Grades are our own judgement for training, not a formal review.

    Why the Stoics fit the evidence. Albert Ellis (REBT) and Aaron Beck (CBT) both drew on Epictetus' idea that we are upset less by events than by our judgements of them. That is the bridge from philosophy to tested therapy. Stoic Week surveys look positive, but they are self-selected and uncontrolled, so treat them as weak evidence.

    Run it

    The daily loop and weekly huddle

    Our Stoic loop (morning premeditation, one rep at a time, evening review), now with the best-supported drill from each book. Ticks save in your browser only.

    Load rule. If your energy has sat under 5 out of 10 for two weeks, take load off before adding any new habit. That is temperance, not weakness. Raise it in your 1:1.

    Try it

    Self-talk re-take

    Goggins records his self-talk and re-records it like a coach. The research backs the re-take: talking to yourself by name or as "you" calms stress (Kross), self-compassion after a miss lifts effort to improve (Breines and Chen), and a short cue helps the next rep (Hatzigeorgiadis).

    Swap these

    Never do a discomfort drill when you are ill, injured, short on sleep or highly stressed. Overtraining and burnout are real (Meeusen and colleagues, 2013 consensus). Goggins-style practices are for staff who opt in. We do not use them with clients.

    The Estus way

    Safe and neuroaffirming use

    Much of our team is neurodivergent. Direct research on habit formation in autistic and ADHD adults is thin, so some of this is reasoned from what we do know.

    Do

    • Lead with structurePut cues where the work happens (Barkley's "point of performance"). It matches Clear and Duckworth, and suits ADHD brains well.
    • Use "never miss twice"A single miss did not derail habits in Lally's study. Flexible ranges beat perfect streaks.
    • Follow interestTemptation bundling and glad-you-did-it activities work with interest-based motivation.
    • Respect sensory needsAdjust the environment (PEO) rather than mask. A cold shower may be overload, not growth.
    • Write norms downExplicit rules make truthseeking groups safer for everyone.

    Don't

    • Say "try harder"It shames, and it is weak advice even for neurotypical people.
    • Use harsh self-talkMany neurodivergent people already carry years of criticism. Distanced, kind, instructional talk works better.
    • Make streaks the goalAll-or-nothing streaks can trigger all-or-nothing thinking.
    • Make the grind a norm"Imbalanced" and "obsessed" are not team values. Temperance limits the grind.
    • Hand this to clientsThese drills are for us. Client work goes through supervision.
