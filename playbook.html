Our learning method

Three ideas, used together

The first two say how to practise. The third says when each one fits.

Active recall

You learn by pulling it out of your head and doing it, not by re-reading.

No scripts in hand during practice. Say it, do it, out loud.

Errorless learning

Set it up so the learner gets it right from the start. Show it first, catch mistakes straight away, repeat the right version.

Best when a mistake is costly or likely to stick.

Kind vs wicked

Kind: clear rules, fast honest feedback. Practice teaches the right lesson. Wicked: fuzzy rules, late or misleading feedback. Experience can teach the wrong lesson.

The Estus Session

Every training block runs the same five phases. Train in groups, not 1-on-1: five people cost about the same trainer time as one.

  1. Kudos5 MIN

    Do

    Name 2 or 3 specific things people did well this week. Then state the one skill we're training today.

    Say it like

    "Nam kept the parent on track by summarising before booking."

  2. Model5 MIN

    Do

    The trainer runs it start to finish, the way we want it. Then runs it again, calling out the key moves.

    Rule

    Nobody tries it until they've seen it done right.

  3. Practise10 MIN

    Do

    Pairs. One plays the clinician, one plays the client or referrer. Everyone practises at once.

    During a good run

    Quiet signals only: thumbs up, a nod, a chat reaction. No talking over it.

  4. Correct + lock in10 TO 15 MIN

    Do

    Trainer moves between pairs. A mistake gets stopped on the spot, fixed, then locked in with 3 clean reps.

    See

    The correction loop

  5. Close5 MIN

    Do

    One pair shows it to the group in 60 seconds. Log the skill as done in the onboarding tracker.

    Then

    Book the live check: a call review or an observed session.

Telehealth version. Breakout rooms for pairs. Signals become emoji reactions. The trainer drops into rooms to correct.

Fix it in the moment

Correction and lock in

Waiting until the end means the learner practises the mistake the whole way through, then gets a list of 17 things to remember. We stop it on the spot instead.

1StopGood. Go back to [the line].
2One fixThis time, [one change]. Try again.
3Re-try the stepJust that step, with the fix.
4Lock it inNice, lock it in.3 clean reps in a row.
5Back inResume the full run from just before the step.

Not right yet: re-try with the fix

  • One fix at a timeNever stack corrections.
  • Be specific"Slow down on the price" beats "sound more confident".
  • Fix the stepNot the person.
  • Three missesSame step fails 3 times: the trainer models it again.

Neuroaffirming adaptations

This method came from field-service training. We keep the engine and adjust the parts that can overload or exclude people.

Part of the methodRiskEstus adjustment
Public kudosSpotlight can feel unsafeAsk each person how they like recognition: out loud, in writing, or 1-on-1
Quiet signalsConstant nodding can distractAgree signals before practice. "No signals" is a valid choice
Stopping mid-runCan feel like a judgementAgree upfront that stops are the method, not a verdict. Anyone can call a pause
Roleplay in front of peersPerformance anxietyFirst run can be with the trainer only. Watching a round first is fine
Learning a script liveProcessing timeSend the script and model recording the day before
3 reps to lock inCan feel drilledExplain the why. Reps can be spoken, typed or shown

Honesty note. Claims that stopping an error "cuts off the neural pathway" are overstated. We use the stop, fix and lock-in loop because it works in practice and keeps feedback fast.

Judgement, not scripts

Wicked skills

Scripts get you to the door. The job is judgement: reading a family, choosing goals, writing an opinion that holds up. Feedback there is slow and noisy, so we build our own feedback loops.

Revisit outcomesIn case review, look back at a call from weeks ago. What did we predict? What happened?
Try the cheap versionRun a draft past a senior clinician before the real thing goes out.
Scope in writingWhen rules are fuzzy, ask a yes or no question in writing before acting.
Label for the readerPractice reports say so on page one: "Practice case study. Not a clinical opinion." No signature block.
Silence is missing dataNo reply is not a verdict. Don't over-learn from one vague signal.

Case lesson: practice reports

A practice assessment report was written to test a template. It included diagnoses, a fitness opinion and instructor language. The reader saw a real report, not a test. Intent doesn't travel; the document does. Train high-stakes work errorless and confirm scope first.
