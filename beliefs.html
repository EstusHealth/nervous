Core beliefs

The deep stories a person holds about themselves, other people and the world. They sit under everyday thoughts. They shift slowly, and mostly through doing.

Beck's cognitive model, simply

Three layers

Automatic thoughts are the surface. Under them sit rules for living. Under those sit core beliefs: broad, rigid, and often learned early. They feel like facts, not opinions.

1Core beliefBroad statements about me, other people and the world. Usually unspoken.I'm a failure.
2Rules and assumptionsHow a person lives with the belief. Often "if... then..." or "I must..."If I don't do it perfectly, everyone will see I'm a failure.
3Automatic thoughtsQuick and tied to the moment. The part people say out loud.I'm going to stuff this up.
Early experienceTold to "try harder", again and again
Core belief"I'm lazy"
Rule"If I rest, I'm being lazy"
SituationWiped out after school
Thought and action"Push through." Skips rest, then crashes

Every crashlooks like more proof

A belief works like a prejudice. Christine Padesky's picture: evidence that fits the belief gets noticed and kept. Evidence that doesn't fit gets missed, explained away or called a fluke. That's why one good day doesn't shift it, and why we collect new evidence on purpose.
Scope. Core belief work goes deep. Estus OTs notice beliefs, name them gently, and help the person gather new evidence through doing, inside OT goals. We are not delivering psychological therapy. Where beliefs are tied to trauma, depression, self-harm, eating, or very high distress, we refer to a psychologist and work alongside them.

Judith Beck's groupings

Three themes

Most negative core beliefs about the self fall into three groups. Knowing the theme helps you hear the same belief across very different situations.

Not capable enough

Helpless

  • "I'm useless."
  • "I can't cope."
  • "I'm a failure."
  • "I'm not as good as everyone else."

Shows up around tasks, school, work and independence.

Not wanted enough

Unlovable

  • "No one likes me."
  • "I'll be rejected."
  • "I'm too different to be wanted."
  • "People leave."

Shows up around friends, family and belonging.

Not good enough

Worthless

  • "I'm bad."
  • "I'm a waste of space."
  • "I'm a burden."
  • "I don't deserve good things."

Shows up as shame. "I'm a burden" always gets a risk check.

Beliefs about others and the world matter too. "People can't be trusted." "The world isn't made for people like me." For neurodivergent clients these can be accurate reads of real experience. Validate the true part first.

Neurodivergent experience

Taught beliefs

Many neurodivergent people were misread for years before anyone named the real reason. So the belief usually makes sense. It was taught. Our job is a fairer story, not a cheerier one.

The beliefWhere it often comes fromA fairer story (in their words, not ours)
"I'm lazy"Years of "try harder" when starting, switching or finishing was the hard part."Starting is hard for my brain. With the right set-up, I work hard."
"I'm stupid"School set-ups that tested speed, handwriting or sitting still, not thinking."School tested some skills. It didn't measure how smart I am."
"I'm too much"Being shushed for excitement, info-dumping, stimming or big feelings."I'm a lot, and the right people like that. Some places fit me better."
"Something is wrong with me"Being measured against a neurotypical ruler, over and over."My brain works differently. Different isn't broken."
"People don't like me"Real rejection, and misunderstandings that go both ways (double empathy)."Some people didn't get me. My people do. I'm still finding them."
"I'm a burden"Needing more help than siblings or classmates, sometimes being told so."Everyone relies on other people. Needing support is human." Check for risk.
"I have to mask to be accepted"Real social costs of being visibly different. Often a safety strategy that worked."Some places aren't safe to unmask yet. I can find the ones that are."
"I'm a burden" and "they'd be better off without me" are risk signals. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts in the same session. Asking does not put the idea in someone's head. Write it up and talk to your supervisor the same day. If someone is in immediate danger, call 000. Lifeline is 13 11 14, any time.
Late diagnosis. A diagnosis, at any age, can be the first fair evidence against an old belief. "I'm lazy" becomes "I was doing hard things without the right supports." Give it time. Grief and relief often turn up together.

Try it

Downward arrow

Start with a hot thought. Don't challenge it. Ask what it would mean if it were true, then ask again. Do it together, at their pace.

Listen for

What you hearWhat it may point to
"I always..." or "I never..."A rigid, global belief, not a one-off.
The same theme in different placesSchool, friends and home all come back to "I'm not good enough". That's a core belief.
"I'm the kind of person who..."An identity-level belief. Ask how they came to know that about themselves.
A big feeling over a small eventSomething deeper got touched. Get curious about what it meant to them.
"I have to..." or "I must..."A rule that protects a belief. Ask what would happen if they didn't.
Stop when you reach a short, broad "I am..." statement, when the answers start to repeat, or when distress climbs. Then check it with them: "Does that sound right?" Finding the belief is enough for one session. Shifting it comes later.

Belief continuum

Core beliefs are all or nothing. A continuum turns them into degrees. Name the quality they want, not the belief they fear. Define both ends in real detail first, then place other people, and the client last. Most people move once the 0% end is described honestly.

Evidence log

The old belief filters out new evidence, so we collect it on purpose. Padesky calls this a positive data log. The new belief should be believable, not perfect.

Evidence for the new belief

Make it stick. Small things count. Write them down the same day, or the belief will explain them away. Rate the new belief weekly, not daily, and expect slow change. Read the log back before a hard task.

How OTs use it

Beliefs change through doing

Talking can loosen a belief. Doing changes it. Bandura found mastery experiences are the strongest source of self-efficacy: doing the thing yourself, and seeing that you did. That's OT's home ground.

Grade it to land

Pick a step they will very likely get. A failed experiment feeds the old belief. Make it smaller, not harder.

Fix the set-up first

Environment over willpower. A set-up that fits their brain gives fair evidence. A set-up that doesn't just repeats history.

Problem solving →

Use their interests

Skill in a special interest is real evidence. The kid who runs a Minecraft build is planning, sequencing and finishing.

Test the rule

Turn a rule into a behavioural experiment. "If I ask for help, they'll think I'm useless." Ask once, then compare.

The Estus way

Core beliefs, neuroaffirming

The aim is a fair, flexible story about themselves. Not a neurotypical story, and not forced positivity.

Do

  • Ask where it came from"Who taught you that?" Naming the source separates the belief from the person.
  • Validate the true part"People don't get me" may be true in some places. Look for where it isn't.
  • Use their wordsThe new belief has to sound like them, or it won't stick.
  • Go slowCore beliefs are tender. If distress climbs, stop and come back to the body first.

Don't

  • Push affirmations they don't believe"I'm amazing" can feel false and make low self-worth worse. Aim for believable.
  • Aim for "normal"The goal isn't passing as neurotypical. "Different, not broken" beats "just like everyone else".
  • Argue them out of itDebate digs beliefs in. Evidence from doing shifts them.
  • Dig into traumaIf the arrow leads to trauma, contain it, ground them, and refer.
